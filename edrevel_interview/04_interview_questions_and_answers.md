## Q1: "Walk me through how you would approach a discovery workshop with a new client who wants to implement Edrevel for compliance training."

**Why they ask this:**
The BA role is fundamentally about discovery — can you extract real requirements from people who often don't know how to articulate them? Do you have a structured process or do you wing it?

**Full Answer:**

> "I run discovery in three phases, and I'm very deliberate about the sequence — because if you jump to solutions before you fully understand the pain, you waste everyone's time.
>
> **Phase 0 — Pre-Workshop Preparation (before the client meeting):**
> I send a pre-read questionnaire 3 days before: 'How many employees? How many learning programs? What systems are you currently using? What's the trigger for this project — audit, leadership priority, system change?' This means I walk in already knowing the basics and can use the workshop time for depth, not basics.
>
> **Phase 1 — Current State (AS-IS): 'How does this work today?'**
> I ask the compliance officer or L&D manager to walk me through the end-to-end process as if I've never seen it before. I literally say: 'Pretend I'm a new employee. I just joined. What happens with my compliance training, from Day 1, through to the point where you have evidence I'm certified?'
> I'm listening for: manual steps, handoffs between teams, tools being used (especially Excel trackers), pain points they mention in passing, time delays.
> I map this in real-time on Miro (if virtual) or a whiteboard — so they can see and correct my understanding.
>
> **Phase 2 — Pain Points: 'What breaks, slows down, or worries you?'**
> I ask direct questions: 'What do you dread about audit preparation?' 'When does your team lose the most time?' 'What's the first thing you'd fix if you had a magic wand?'
> This is where you hear the real business pain — not the technical requirements, but what's actually costing them time, money, stress, and risk.
>
> **Phase 3 — Success Definition: 'What does great look like?'**
> 'If we implemented Edrevel and everything went perfectly — what would your week look like in 12 months that it doesn't look like today?'
> This question reveals their version of success — which is what I design toward.
>
> **After the workshop:**
> I document the findings in an AS-IS → TO-BE map, prioritize requirements using MoSCoW, and produce a Solution Brief within 48 hours while it's still fresh. I include a section called 'What We Heard' which mirrors back their exact language — this builds enormous trust because they feel understood.
> I schedule a 30-minute review call to confirm the brief before any work starts."

## Q2: "How would you explain Edrevel's AI Course Creator to a CFO who is skeptical about AI?"

**Full Answer:**

> "I'd never start with the technology. I'd start with the pain.
>
> I'd say: 'Let me ask you something — how much does it cost your business every time your L&D team needs to update a mandatory compliance course? Think about GDPR, SOX, your internal policies. How often does that change, and how long does the update take?'
>
> When they say '6-8 weeks and an outside vendor,' I'd say: 'So every time regulations change, you're exposed for 6-8 weeks with outdated training. And your team is in the weeds of content production instead of strategy.'
>
> Then I introduce the solution — not as 'AI,' but as: 'What if your L&D team could produce a first-draft compliance course in under 2 hours?'
>
> THEN I explain how: 'Our platform uses the same generation technology behind ChatGPT, but it's been specifically trained on instructional design principles. You give it a policy document, a job role, and the regulatory context. It produces a structured course — modules, knowledge checks, a final assessment — that your team reviews and approves. The AI does the first 80% in 2 hours. Your team does the final 20% over a day. Total time: 2 days instead of 6 weeks.'
>
> Then the ROI: 'If your L&D team's time is worth ₹50,000 per day, and you save 40 working days per course, that's ₹20 lakhs per course, per year in capacity savings. And you've eliminated the exposure window.'
>
> The last thing I say: 'The AI doesn't replace your compliance expertise. It handles the grunt work so your experts can spend their time on what actually requires human judgment.'
>
> CFOs respond to two things: risk elimination and cost reduction. That's the frame."

## Q3: "What's the difference between an LMS and an LXP, and why does it matter for clients considering Edrevel?"

**Full Answer:**

> "The LMS was built for administrators and for compliance — it answers the question 'did this person complete this required course?' It's top-down: admin pushes content to employees, employees complete it because they have to, system records it. It was designed in an era when L&D's job was simply to deploy training and prove it happened. Think Moodle, Cornerstone, SAP SuccessFactors Learning.
>
> The LXP was built for learners and for growth — it answers a different question: 'what does this person need to learn next to become better at their job?' It's bottom-up: the platform personalizes content based on your role, your goals, your skill gaps, and your learning history. Think Netflix for professional development. Think Degreed, EdCast, LinkedIn Learning.
>
> Here's why the distinction matters for a Edrevel client: every organization I've talked to has BOTH problems simultaneously. They have mandatory compliance training that MUST be tracked and completed (they need an LMS). AND they have a business need to develop skills, retain talent, and remain competitive (they need an LXP). Historically, you had to buy two platforms. Or sacrifice one for the other.
>
> Edrevel's positioning is that it solves both in a single platform. The SkillsPro engine and AI Course Creator are LXP capabilities — personalized, intelligent, engagement-driven. The compliance tracking, role-based assignments, certificate management, and audit reporting are LMS capabilities.
>
> For a client, this means: one platform, one budget, one integration point with HR, one dashboard to look at. The compliance officer and the L&D director both get what they need. That's the business case."

## Q4: "A client calls you 6 weeks after go-live. Their engagement is 22%, well below the target of 60%. How do you handle this call and what do you do next?"

**Full Answer — step by step:**

> "My first response on the call is NOT to be defensive or to immediately suggest solutions. It's to listen and ask questions.
>
> I'd say: 'Thank you for calling me directly — let's figure this out together. Before we jump to fixes, can I spend 48 hours pulling the data so I can give you an accurate diagnosis rather than a guess? The last thing I want to do is recommend something that doesn't fix the real problem.'
>
> Then I'd pull the Insights Dashboard and look at 6 dimensions:
>
> **1. Device split:** Are the active 22% using mobile or desktop? If 80% of logins are at lunchtime on mobile, but your content is desktop-only PDFs — there's your problem.
>
> **2. Department breakdown:** Is 22% spread evenly, or is it 5 departments at 80% and 8 departments at 3%? If it's clustered — it's a manager adoption problem in specific teams, not a platform problem.
>
> **3. Content engagement:** Which courses have the highest drop-off? Where in the course do people abandon it? A 45-minute video with a 90% drop-off at minute 8 tells me the content is too long or irrelevant.
>
> **4. Technical friction:** Is SSO working properly? Are push notifications configured? Are users getting emails they can action? Sometimes engagement is low simply because people keep forgetting the platform exists and there's no nudge system in place.
>
> **5. Manager involvement:** Are managers themselves using the platform? If a team manager has never logged in, their team almost certainly hasn't either.
>
> **6. Content freshness:** When was the most recent course added? If nothing new has been added since go-live, there's no reason for learners to come back.
>
> Then I'd build a Diagnostic Report — not a problem report, a diagnostic — with the root cause in plain English and 3 specific interventions ranked by impact-to-effort ratio. I'd present it on a 30-minute call within 48 hours.
>
> And I'd close with a 30-day sprint plan — concrete changes, specific metrics, review date — so the client feels they have a partner with a plan, not a vendor making excuses."

## Q5: "How do you prioritize when a client requests a feature that doesn't exist in Edrevel?"

**Full Answer:**

> "This happens frequently, and how you handle it determines whether you're a vendor or a trusted advisor. There's a very specific process I follow.
>
> **Step 1: Understand the real need behind the request.**
> The stated feature request is almost never the actual business need. A client saying 'we need bulk CSV upload for course assignments' is really saying 'our HR system doesn't integrate with you yet and manually assigning courses to 3,000 people is killing us.' If you solve the real need (HRIS integration), the feature request becomes irrelevant.
> I always ask: 'Tell me more about the workflow this would support. What's the problem you're trying to solve?' 3 out of 5 times, the answer reveals a different — and available — solution.
>
> **Step 2: Audit current capabilities thoroughly.**
> Before I say 'we can't do that,' I check with the product team. Sometimes features exist but aren't well-documented. Sometimes there's an admin setting that achieves 80% of what they need. Sometimes a partner integration covers it.
>
> **Step 3: Offer a bridging workaround.**
> If it genuinely doesn't exist: 'Here's what we can do today that gets you 70% of the way there. It's slightly manual, but it works while we evaluate the full feature.' Clients appreciate honesty and creativity much more than empty promises.
>
> **Step 4: Document and escalate strategically.**
> I write up the request as a product feedback ticket with: the client name, the use case, the business impact if we had it, and a note if I've heard the same request from other clients. Product teams prioritize based on patterns across multiple customers — one customer's request is noise; five customers' same request is signal.
>
> **Step 5: Set explicit expectations.**
> 'This is not on the roadmap for Q1. I've submitted it as a priority request. I'll update you when I have clarity on timeline.' Never promise what you can't deliver. Broken promises destroy trust faster than any feature gap."

## Q6: "How do you build a business case or proposal for a client?"

**Full Answer with structure:**

> "A good proposal is not a feature brochure. It's a mirror — it reflects the client's own pain back at them and shows them a path out.
>
> Here's the structure I use, and why each section exists:
>
> **Section 1: Executive Summary (1 page)**
> Written for the person who may not read the rest. Three things: what problem you have, what we're proposing, what outcome you can expect. If the executive skims this and wants to know more, the proposal worked.
>
> **Section 2: Your Current Situation (The Pain)**
> This section should contain the client's own words from discovery. Quote them back: 'During our discovery session, you shared that audit preparation currently takes 3 people, 2 weeks, and relies on a spreadsheet that's never fully accurate.' When they read their own words, they feel understood — and the problem feels more urgent.
> Quantify where possible: 'You currently have 2 compliance officers spending 40% of their time on manual tracking. That's 32 hours per week — roughly 1,600 hours per year — on work that is entirely automatable.'
>
> **Section 3: Proposed Solution (Mapped to Their Pain)**
> Never say 'Edrevel has feature X.' Say 'The audit report problem is solved by...' Each pain point gets mapped to a specific feature. I use a two-column table: Their Pain / Our Solution.
>
> **Section 4: Implementation Roadmap**
> Visual, phased, with dates. Clients need to visualize what they're committing to. Include their responsibilities too — a successful implementation requires their participation (content provision, admin access, user communication). If they don't see their role in the plan, they'll be surprised by it later.
>
> **Section 5: ROI Model**
> Keep it simple — one page, three numbers. Cost of their current state, cost of Edrevel, net value. Use their own data where possible (ask for it in discovery). A conservative ROI estimate that uses their real numbers is 10x more persuasive than an optimistic one built from industry averages.
>
> **Section 6: Next Steps with Specific Dates**
> 'To meet your audit deadline of [date], we need to begin implementation by [date]. Our proposed next step is to schedule a kickoff call for [specific date]. Is this timeline workable?'
> Vague next steps = deal stalls. Specific next steps = deals close."

## Q7: "What do you know about current AI trends in EdTech and L&D?"

**Full Answer:**

> "I see three major waves happening simultaneously:
>
> **Wave 1 — Generative AI for Content Creation (here now):**
> AI generating courses, assessments, and learning materials from source documents. This is what Edrevel's AI Course Creator does. Every serious L&D platform is building this. The competitive question is: how good is the quality, how fast is it, and how much customization do you need post-generation? The winning platforms will make the 20% human refinement step as easy as possible.
>
> **Wave 2 — AI Tutors and Conversational Learning (emerging):**
> Instead of passively watching a course, learners interact with an AI tutor that answers questions, explains concepts differently when they're confused, gives hints on assessments, and adapts the experience in real-time. Think Khanmigo (Khan Academy's AI tutor) but for corporate training. This is 2-3 years from mainstream enterprise adoption — but platforms that get there first will have a massive engagement advantage.
>
> **Wave 3 — Skills Intelligence (early stages):**
> AI that infers skills from unstructured data — not just 'completed this course,' but 'wrote this code,' 'led this project,' 'resolved this customer complaint.' Connecting learning to work outputs is the holy grail of L&D analytics. When that bridge is fully built, organizations can genuinely know their skill capability in real-time — not as a self-reported survey but as a dynamic, continuously updated map.
>
> What I find most interesting is that the real competitive battle isn't in the technology itself — every platform can access the same LLMs. The battle is in: quality of the learning design layer on top of AI, accuracy of the skills taxonomy that powers personalization, and the quality of the data that makes recommendations trustworthy.
>
> Edrevel's positioning with SkillsPro — if the skills data is high quality — puts it ahead of platforms that have AI content creation but lack the personalization engine to use it intelligently."
